# Source 06 — "My investment research process" (Richard Coffin, The Plain Bagel)

Sponsored by Morning Brew (newsletter signup link). Author states he works as a
portfolio manager / investment analyst.

## Caveats stated up front

- "This isn't a recommendation that people should necessarily stock pick... I
  think most people are better served putting their efforts elsewhere, either
  hiring a professional or just investing passively. Countless studies have shown
  just how difficult it is to beat the market with stock picking."
- Names three research families: technical analysis (past prices), quantitative
  analysis (relationships between variables), fundamental analysis (his own).
  Within fundamental: tilts to value investing, holds some growth.
- The process is explicitly informal and changed over time. "The whole research
  process takes quite a long time — it has taken weeks at times."

## Step 1 — Screen for ideas

A screener filters the universe to companies meeting criteria. His filters are
"pretty broad": **revenue growth**, **being profitable**, **debt within a certain
range**. Tickers then go through an internal one-page summary tool covering past
financial performance, consensus estimates, debt levels — not to decide, just to
see whether it's worth a deep dive.

Also admits to researching stocks simply because of interest or a recent
development, with no screen involved.

## Step 2 — Understand the business (qualitative)

Take the mindset of a part business owner. Understand: all segments and what
they do, how they earn money, whether revenues are contractual / recurring /
seasonal, geography, customers, suppliers, employees, regulators, management.
Note sector-specific KPIs so peers can be compared.

States a hard boundary: "there are times where I don't research a company
because I don't yet have that familiarity with the industry" — circle of
competence.

Sources: Google search; **annual report / 10-K**; most recent quarterly; EDGAR
(US) or SEDAR (Canada); investor relations page; investor reports; **recent 8-K
filings**; paid external analyst research; commercials, tutorials, customer
reviews.

Notes taken under the **SWOT** framework (strengths/weaknesses internal,
opportunities/threats external), plus a "parking lot" of open questions.

Goal: find **quality companies** — competitive advantage, attractive business
model, a Buffett-style moat.

## Step 3 — Understand the finances

Looks at financial position (debt, solvency, capital structure, ability to meet
payments), growth profile, profitability.

Three statements: income statement (accounting earnings), balance sheet (owns vs
owes, a snapshot), cash flow statement (where cash actually moved).

Specific techniques named:

- **CAGRs computed over 5, 10 and 15 year periods** for revenue and profit
  measures.
- **Common-size statements** — every line as a percentage of a key figure
  (revenue for the income statement, total assets for the balance sheet), to see
  which costs grow or shrink relative to the total.
- Margins and ratios for profitability, operational, solvency, liquidity.
- **Peer analysis** against companies in the same space on those financials plus
  the KPIs from step 2. "I have at times pivoted to a new idea based on this peer
  comparison."

### Forensic / red-flag check (runs throughout)

Related-party transactions, management compensation, conflicts of interest, and
accounting shenanigans. Specific warning on **adjusted figures**: management
takes an accounting figure and changes it. "It's really important to look into
what the definition of that adjusted figure is and what they are adding or taking
out of this number, because the reason it's adjusted is that it doesn't meet
accounting rules or regulations."

Data sourcing caveat: relies on a paid data terminal; free versions exist but
"make sure you understand how the data is pulled, whether it's aggregated,
whether they make any adjustments to it."

## Step 4 — Understand the strategy (forward-looking)

Management's strategic priorities, planned **capital expenditure**, and how it
will be financed (debt, share issuance, hybrids). Evaluates feasibility, risks,
and **track record of doing what they said they would**. Specific caution on
serial acquirers: prefer those with a history of actually improving acquired
businesses rather than "spending investor money to put growth on the accounting
statements."

Track record measured via 5/10/15-year averages of **ROI figures — return on
capital employed, return on invested capital, return on equity** — plus
qualitative review of management experience and tenure.

Suggests building a personal **checklist** that grows as you research. Example
item given: does the company add value for all stakeholders, or do stakeholders
outside of competition lose from its operations?

Sources: 10-K strategic objectives, investor presentations, **earnings call
transcripts** (management answers on expectations and challenges), conference
transcripts, and — via his employer — direct executive meetings. For industry
research: news, total addressable market stats.

## Step 5 — Valuation (deliberately last)

Left late "because I don't want my earlier steps to be influenced by whether the
stock is expensive or cheap."

- **Relative valuation:** multiples — P/E, EV/EBITDA, free cash flow yield —
  compared both to the company's own history and to peers.
- **Absolute valuation:** discounted cash flow, forecasting line items to get
  future free cash flow, discounted to today. Varies the inputs to see how the
  justified price changes.
- Also reviews current events, lawsuits, headlines, press releases — "it tends to
  be the more recent stuff that sways prices in the short term. It doesn't matter
  much as a long-term investor, but it does help with the entry point."
- Considers "some technical aspects" and whether management is buying.

Stated problem: companies that survive all earlier filters "tend to be the most
expensive, because investors recognize that and they bid up the stock price
accordingly." If not attractively valued, it goes on a **watch list** — research
is already done, so he can act quickly on a dip and judge whether a current event
is material. Will also dollar-cost average into companies he really likes, "but
I always have an eye on valuation — paying too much for a stock does increase
your risk of capital impairment."

## Step 6 — Review and finalise notes

Answer the parked questions. Deliberately **play devil's advocate**: find
conflicting reports and opposing opinions to sanity-check assumptions. Share with
a more experienced team. Write a formal report, or at minimum organise notes into
bullets and a summary.

Stated reason this step matters: when something negative develops, "you can go
back and see what the fundamental business is and what you believe the value lies
in... that will make it easier to evaluate whether that has fundamentally changed
or if this new development is just noise."

Closes by noting the process is ongoing — keep reviewing after investing.
