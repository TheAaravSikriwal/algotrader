# Source 03 — "Federal Reserve chessboard" investment strategy (YouTube)

Stated caveats by the author: this is their own strategy, not a recommendation;
they do not short-term trade; horizon is "at least 3 months up to a few years";
they adjust portfolio weight and stock type to take profits "in a few months or
two years maximum."

## The two monitored variables

1. **Federal Reserve interest rate** (low/falling vs high/rising)
2. **Federal Reserve balance sheet** (increasing = QE, decreasing = QT)

## Stated mechanism

Lower rates reduce WACC through both components: cost of debt falls directly,
and cost of equity falls because the risk-free rate (US Treasury yield) falls.
A lower WACC raises the present value of future cash flows, so valuations and
P/E multiples rise. Worked example given: $100 next year at 10% discounts to
$90.9; at 8% it discounts to $92.6.

Second channel: lower bond yields push investors out of bonds into stocks,
raising demand for equities.

Third channel: lower rates reduce debt-servicing burden. "The companies which
are growing and expanding with a lot of debt with not much money because
they're not generating profits will benefit the most."

Balance sheet increasing (QE): banks receive cash for bonds, become eager to
lend, borrowing becomes easy and cheap. Balance sheet decreasing (QT): the
opposite — harder to borrow, higher borrowing rate.

## The four stock archetypes

Four metrics used: revenue growth, earnings growth, forward P/E, debt/EBITDA.

| Stock | Rev growth | Earnings growth | Forward P/E | Debt/EBITDA |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5% | 5% | 10x | 1x |
| B | 10% | 10% | 20x | 3x |
| C | 20% | 10% | 25x | 5x |
| D | >50% (80–100%) | loss-making | n/a | negative EBITDA, "huge debt" |

Explicit instruction on P/E: use **forward**, not trailing, because investors
are forward-looking and historical earnings can be distorted by one-off items.

Debt/EBITDA is used as a leverage proxy — "how many years the company will take
to pay down its debt" — and matters more now because refinancing ease depends
on the Fed environment.

## The quadrant rules

- **Q4 — rates high/rising, balance sheet decreasing (least liquid).** Invest
  no more than 20% of cash, or sit out. If forced to choose: **stock A**. Low
  leverage keeps it solvent; already-low multiple means WACC increases hurt it
  less. Author notes high-multiple stocks trigger "selloff mentality."
- **Q3 — rates low/falling, balance sheet decreasing.** No more than 50% of
  cash. Prefers **stock C**: high growth gets valued, already has the debt it
  needs so only refinances rather than borrows new, high multiple justified
  while WACC falls.
- **Q2 — rates high/rising, balance sheet increasing.** No more than 50% of
  cash. Prefers **stock B**: some leverage but less than C, multiple high but
  lower than C, so WACC damage is contained.
- **Q1 — rates falling AND QE (most liquid).** "I will super aggressively invest
  into the market. I mean, I'll not only invest 100% of my cash, but if possible,
  even take on loans to invest." Buy **stock D** — excessive growth, no earnings.
  "This is the time when the companies rarely go bankrupt."

## Stated exit rule for Q1

"You really, really, really need to exit the investment at the right time. When
you feel like you gained a lot of profit or when you feel like there's any and
I mean any signs of the interest rate going back up or the quantitative easing
ending, you have to sell the stock right away. Don't get swayed... Just sell.
20% profit, 50% profit, 100% profit, 200% profit. These are all great."

## Closing notes given

1. A stock with D's growth and A's valuation and leverage "would be the best in
   all scenarios", but few exist.
2. Compare a stock against peers within the same sector.
3. "Don't chase for fast money." Expect to hold 6 months to 2 years.
