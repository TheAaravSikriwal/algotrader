# Source 08 — "How to make money investing in stocks" (Mark Tilbury)

Opens with a personal story: his father kept savings in a shoebox and a
low-interest bank account, thought the stock market was risky, and lost purchasing
power to inflation instead. Author states "I'm no financial advisor but I am
someone that's been there and done it", and claims his investments "grow by
around $117,000 a week on their own."

Monetisation: sponsored segment for **Trading 212**, referral code "TILB", free
share worth up to £100, links in description. Also a "pies" feature and a
paper-trading feature promoted.

## The inflation hurdle

"In the last 60 years this averages out to a rate of **3.8% per year**. So if
your money isn't growing by more than this on its own then you're getting poorer
by the second."

Target: 8–10% annual return. "Such savings accounts don't exist" — you get it
from the stock market.

## Two ways a shareholder earns

1. Price appreciation (sell for more than you paid).
2. **Dividends** — regular payments to shareholders, money without selling. Not
   all stocks pay them.

## Compounding claim

"If you were able to invest **$250 per month at an 8% annual return, in 42 years
you'd be a millionaire**, and if you continue for another 10 years you would have
over $2 million." Stated as historical average data, not guaranteed.

"The real secret ingredient to this millionaire formula is **time**."

## Prerequisites before investing (stated order)

1. **Pay off high-interest debt** first. "There's no point trying to make 10% in
   the stock market if you're paying 15% to a credit card company."
2. **Build an emergency fund covering 3–6 months of living expenses**, so you are
   never forced to sell stocks in an emergency.
3. Under 18: ask a parent to open a **custodial account**.

## How much to invest

Rejects "as much as possible." Says he made most of his money starting
businesses and used the market only to grow wealth, and that investing everything
would have cost him experiences.

Offers the **70/20/10 rule**: 70% living expenses, 20% investments, 10% fun.
"Research shows that people who invest at this level are much better equipped to
ride the ups and downs of life."

## Account types

Tax-advantaged wrappers named by country: **Roth IRA** (US), **stocks and shares
ISA** (UK), **TFSA** (Canada), **super** (Australia). "They allow you to avoid
paying taxes on your investments, but they do have limits because they're so
powerful."

**Fractional shares** let you buy $1 of a $190 stock.

## Technical vs fundamental

Presents them as a scale. "Usually short-term day traders are purely focused on
the technical aspects — charts and patterns... they believe they can predict how
the stock will change in price by judging the highs and the lows on the graphs."

His own stance: focus on **fundamentals — financials, leadership, brand
recognition**. Holding intent of "at least 2 to 5 years." Does look at "the
occasional chart in order to find the best time to buy." Explicitly does not use
margin or options.

## The core recommendation: index funds

Analogy: a sports league table, with companies instead of teams — poor performers
get dropped. The S&P 500 is ~500 of the largest US public companies. Buying the
index means one click buys all of them, so a few failures are offset.

Claims made:

- **"The average annual return of the S&P 500 over the last 10 years has been
  13.6%"** — acknowledged as "slightly higher than the average over time."
- **"No one has ever lost any money if they bought and held an S&P 500 index fund
  for more than 20 years."**
- **"The average actively managed fund returned 2% less per year than the market
  in general."** Professionals underperform and still charge fees regardless.
- Index funds are passively managed so **fees can be as low as 0.2% per year**.

### The three funds he names

**1. S&P 500 tracker.** Historical return quoted as 8–10%. Notes the index covers
11 sectors with no sector above 30%, but is "a bit tech heavy these days, with
five tech stocks dominating 23% of the entire fund." Tickers given: VFIAX index
fund or **VOO** ETF (US); **VUSA** ETF (UK).

**2. Total stock market index.** Claims it "has returned investors an average of
13% each year over the last 10 years" [transcript reads "133%" — evidently a
transcription error]. "The definition of diversification." Downside stated: a
single stock you believe in won't move the needle. Tickers: VTSAX / **VTI** (US);
**VWRL** (UK).

**3. Emerging markets index.** Called "the most risky type of index funds we've
discussed", potentially heavy in Chinese companies. Anecdote: a Shenzhen
apartment he looked at ~20 years ago cost $47,000 and "now it's worth over a
million." Tickers: **VWO** (US); **VFEM** (UK).

### Index fund vs ETF

"The only real difference is that the ETF can be purchased or sold at any time
throughout the day, just like a stock." Index funds must be bought in full
shares; ETFs allow fractional, which suits dollar-cost averaging. He uses a
monthly direct debit.

## Is investing risky

"It really depends on how you define risk. You may be scared that you'll lose
money, however you also face this risk by not investing."

Bonds recommended as the stabiliser: "In your 20s and 30s I wouldn't worry too
much about them, but as you move closer to retirement it's a good idea to have
more bonds than stocks." And: "In your younger years the biggest risk you can
take is not taking enough risk."

Crashes he lived through and says all recovered: dot-com, 2008, 2020 COVID.

## When to sell

If older, sell gradually to live off in retirement. In your 20s–30s, **only three
good reasons**:

1. **Emergency** — which the emergency fund should already cover.
2. **A bad investment.** If an individual stock underperforms consistently, cut
   losses — but first check the wider industry. "If everything's doing poorly
   this gives you a bit of extra context."
3. **A specific goal reached.** Set a target price in advance. He does not
   recommend investing for short or medium-term goals.

"The most important thing to remember is to not sell your stocks for as long as
possible... This thinking will also help you avoid panic selling."
